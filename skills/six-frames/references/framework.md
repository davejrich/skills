# The Six Frames: framework reference

Source: Edward de Bono, *Six Frames for Thinking About Information* (2008).

De Bono's point: we're drowning in information but rarely look at it with intent. The six frames are deliberate lenses. You put one frame on at a time, look through it, note what you see, then move on. Same idea as the Six Thinking Hats, but aimed at *taking in* information rather than *generating* ideas.

The order matters. Purpose first (otherwise you can't judge anything else), Outcome last.

## The frames

| # | Frame | Shape | Focus | Core question |
|---|-------|-------|-------|---------------|
| 1 | Triangle | ▲ | Purpose | Why am I looking at this? What do I need from it? |
| 2 | Circle | ● | Accuracy | How true and reliable is it? |
| 3 | Square | ■ | Point of view | Whose angle is this? What angles are missing? |
| 4 | Heart | ♥ | Interest | What's interesting, surprising, or worth noting beyond my purpose? |
| 5 | Diamond | ◆ | Value | What's actually valuable here, and to whom? |
| 6 | Slab | ▬ | Outcome | So what? What do I conclude and do next? |

### 1. Triangle (Purpose)
The three points of the triangle: who wants the information, why, and what exactly is needed. Without this frame you scan everything and keep nothing.
- What decision or action is this information feeding?
- Am I exploring broadly or hunting for a specific answer?
- What would "enough information" look like?

### 2. Circle (Accuracy)
The target. How close to the bullseye is this information?
- Is it fact, estimate, opinion, or rumour?
- Who's the source, and how do they know?
- What evidence backs it up? Can it be checked?
- Rate it: certain / likely / possible / unverified / doubtful.

### 3. Square (Point of view)
Four sides, four viewpoints. Information always comes from somewhere.
- Who produced this, and what do they gain from it?
- What bias, incentive, or worldview is baked in?
- How would a competitor, a customer, a sceptic, or an expert see it?
- What's conspicuously missing?

### 4. Heart (Interest)
What catches attention, regardless of purpose. Useful for spotting things you didn't know to look for.
- What's surprising or counter-intuitive?
- What sparks a new idea or connection?
- What's odd, contradictory, or worth following up later?

### 5. Diamond (Value)
Diamonds are valuable but not everything shiny is a diamond. Separates "interesting" from "useful".
- Which pieces serve the Triangle purpose directly?
- What's valuable to someone else (client, audience, team) even if not to me?
- Value type: saves time, makes money, reduces risk, informs a decision, teaches something.
- What's noise that can be dropped?

### 6. Slab (Outcome)
The solid slab you stand on afterwards. The summary and the "so what".
- What do I now know that I didn't before?
- What's the conclusion, and how confident am I (from Circle)?
- What action, decision, or next question comes out of this?
- If nothing: say so. "No useful outcome" is a valid result.

## Common mistakes
- Skipping Triangle. Then Diamond has nothing to measure value against.
- Mixing Circle and Square. "Is it true?" and "whose view is it?" are different questions. Biased sources can still be accurate.
- Treating Heart as Diamond. Interesting is not the same as valuable.
- Ending without a Slab. Analysis with no outcome is just reading.
