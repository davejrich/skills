# The Six Value Medals: framework reference

Source: Edward de Bono, *The Six Value Medals* (2005). Clay is an addition by this skill, not de Bono's.

De Bono's point: we talk about values constantly but rarely look at them deliberately. The medals split "value" into distinct types so you can see each one clearly, notice what's missing, and spot where they pull against each other. Each medal has a positive side (value created) and a negative side (value lost or put at risk). Look for both.

This skill points the medals at a **business and its founder** rather than at a single decision. The question for each medal is "how much does this business value this, and what does the evidence show?"

## Rating scale

| Rating | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Strong | Clear "done" evidence that this drives decisions. |
| Present | Some "done" evidence, or strong "said" evidence only. |
| Weak | Evidence the business neglects or spends down this value. |
| Missing | No evidence either way. Not the same as Weak. |

"Said" = what they claim. "Done" = what behaviour shows. Behaviour wins.

## The medals

### 🥇 Gold: Human values
How the business affects people: staff, customers, suppliers, the founder's family. Gold is the most important value type for de Bono, because in the end values are about people.
- Look at: reviews mentioning staff by name, how complaints are answered, job ads (pay shown? flexibility? tone?), staff tenure on LinkedIn, team photos and bios.
- Negative side: burnout signs, constant hiring for the same role, customers treated as transactions.
- Interview: "Tell me about someone who's worked with you a long time. Why did they stay?" · "What would you never ask a staff member to do?" · "When a customer is unhappy, what happens?"

### 🥈 Silver: Organisational values
What the organisation itself needs to survive and succeed: profit, cash flow, growth, market share, stability, the business's own goals.
- Look at: pricing position, expansion (new locations, hires, services), how they talk about growth, packages and upsells, ABN age, whether they're hiring.
- Negative side: underpricing, dependence on one client or channel, growth that starves cash.
- Interview: "What does a good year look like in numbers?" · "If you had to cut something tomorrow, what goes first?" · "Where do you want the business in three years?"

### ⚙️ Steel: Quality values
Does it do what it's meant to do, and do it well? Steel is about meeting the intended standard: reliability, craftsmanship, getting it right.
- Look at: licences and certifications, warranties and guarantees, reviews about workmanship and reliability, process descriptions, portfolio quality.
- Negative side: rushed work, callbacks, quality traded away for speed or price.
- Interview: "What does 'a job done properly' mean here?" · "When did you last turn work down or redo it?" · "How do you check quality without being on site?"

### 🔷 Glass: Innovation and creativity values
How much the business values new, simpler or better ways of doing things. Glass is made from simple sand with creativity, so simplicity counts here too.
- Look at: tools and tech they mention, online booking, new services, how current the website is, whether they talk about doing things differently from competitors.
- Negative side: "we've always done it this way", manual processes that obviously hurt.
- Interview: "What's the last thing you changed about how the business runs?" · "What do you do that others in your industry don't?" · "What would you automate if it was easy?"

### 🌳 Wood: Environmental and community values
The impact on people and places outside the business: local community, the environment, the industry, third parties who aren't customers.
- Look at: sponsorships, local involvement, sustainability claims and whether they're backed up, apprenticeships, industry bodies, waste and disposal practices.
- Negative side: complaints from neighbours, greenwashing, community claims with no visible action.
- Interview: "Who outside your customers is affected by what you do?" · "What does the business give back, and why?" · "Is there anything in your industry you'd like to see change?"

### 🔔 Brass: Perception values
How the business wants to be seen and how it is actually seen. Brass looks like gold but isn't. Perception is still a real value: it wins work and loses it.
- Look at: branding consistency, review rating and volume, social presence, awards, how they describe themselves vs how reviewers describe them.
- Negative side: polished image with poor reviews underneath; or great work that nobody can see.
- Interview: "How do you want a new customer to describe you to a friend?" · "What do you think people get wrong about you?" · "Where do your best customers come from?"

### 🏺 Clay: The founder's own stakes (added medal)
What the founder personally wants out of the business. The clay is shaped by the founder's hands. In a small business the founder's personal stakes drive almost every decision, yet they aren't Gold (people affected) or Silver (organisation goals). Clay captures them.
- Covers: lifestyle and hours, control vs delegation, legacy and identity, exit plans (sell, hand on, wind down), risk appetite, money needed personally, what they're proud of.
- Look at (desk, usually thin): founder bio, origin story, how much they appear in the branding, interviews and podcasts, LinkedIn posts, side ventures.
- Negative side: a founder who is the bottleneck, burnout, a business that no longer fits the life they want.
- Interview: "Why did you start this?" · "What would you want your week to look like in three years?" · "What happens to the business when you step back?" · "What's the one decision you'd never hand to someone else?" · "What keeps you up at night?"

**Why it's justified:** de Bono's medals assess the value of an action or organisation, so the owner's personal stake falls between Gold and Silver. In founder-led SMEs, misreading that stake is the most common reason advice gets ignored. A plan that grows Silver but costs the founder Clay (more hours, less control) will be politely declined.

## Common tensions

- Silver growth vs Clay lifestyle: the founder says they want growth but won't give up hours or control.
- Steel quality vs Silver cost: high standards that pricing doesn't pay for.
- Brass image vs Gold reality: a great-looking brand with staff or customer pain underneath.
- Glass novelty vs Steel reliability: new things that keep breaking.
- Wood community vs Silver margin: local commitments that cost more than the business can carry.

## Common mistakes

- Rating on "said" evidence. A mission statement is a Brass signal, not proof of Gold.
- Treating Missing as Weak. No evidence isn't bad evidence.
- Guessing Clay in desk mode. Mark it thin and turn it into interview questions.
- Moralising. The map describes what drives this founder, not what should.
- Ending without a next action. The map exists to make the next conversation better.
